# VOICE

**A style guide for Fathom, distilled from *The Dark Way Down* (2016).**

Source: `dark-way-down-2016.txt` — 50,377 words total, of which ~44,550 are prose (the rest is choice text and programmer notes). 217 story nodes, 39 terminal deaths, unfinished. Read in full. All counts below are measured against the prose body with choice buttons and `///notes///` stripped.

---

## HONEST ASSESSMENT

This is real writing by someone with a real ear, and it is not finished, and the two facts are related. The strengths are specific and unusual: an extraordinary sensitivity to **sound** as the primary channel of dread [see the caveat below on the disputed count]; an instinct for **withholding the source** of a noise for a beat or three; a genuine gift for the concrete physical detail that carries an entire scene's worth of implication (a full cup of tea, still steaming, in a room under a quarter-inch of dust); and a rhythmic control at the paragraph level — he knows how to close on a short flat sentence and let it ring. The best passages here are as good as published Lovecraftian pastiche and better than most of it, because they are more physical and less adjectival. The limits are equally specific. He hedges compulsively — *seem/seems/seemed* 118 times, *perhaps* 52, *however* 53 — and the hedging is a genuine epistemic device in the first ten pages and pure verbal tic by page fifty. He cannot resist a joke at the moment of death, and with 39 deaths the jokes stop being tonal relief and start being an escape hatch: the prose flinches at exactly the point it should hold. Roughly one comparison every 285 words is too many, and about a third of them deflate the register they were meant to serve (a cosmic horror should not be "like the Eiffel Tower"). The manor section is a furniture catalogue in places, and the `A4`–`A22` maze is nineteen nodes of literally identical text — visible scaffolding that was never built on. The overall shape (crawlspace → sewer → spider civilisation → haunted manor → subterranean river → Dante's *Inferno* → Cthulhu) is three good games stapled together, and the seams are why it stalled. **None of that argues against harvesting it.** The floor is high, the ceiling is higher than the average, and the failure mode is entirely diagnosable: too many words per idea, too many jokes per corpse, too much hedging per sentence. Imitate the top decile and delete the crutches, and you get a voice worth building a game on.

## A CAVEAT ON ONE NUMBER — READ THIS BEFORE TRUSTING THE REST

This guide was written by one pass and spot-checked by another. Most claims reproduced exactly: the fish-beings at L4336 and the ripple-reading at L4368 are verbatim; the wrench-on-steel-plate and walnuts-in-a-nutcracker similes are verbatim; the nautical vocabulary is present at the frequencies stated (`quay` 6, `gaff` 5, `bulwark` 3, `pilings` 3); and the emotion-naming discipline holds emphatically — a broad search for *you are/you're/you feel* + an emotion adjective returns **4 hits in 50,000 words**, against the guide's 7. Either way the finding stands and it is the single most valuable observation here.

**The one claim that did NOT reproduce is the sound-versus-sight ratio.** The original pass measured sound 273 / sight 235. An independent count returned sound 177 / sight 276 — the opposite ordering. The two used different token lists (the second counted *look/looks/looked/looking*, which is enormously common as a neutral verb: *you look around*, *it looks like*). Neither list is obviously correct, so **treat the ratio as unproven.**

What survives regardless, and is directly checkable: his threats are routinely introduced as an unattributed *noise*, and his sound similes are distinctively industrial and domestic rather than organic. **Rule 1 stands on that evidence, not on the disputed count.**

## 1. SENTENCE MECHANICS

**Measured across 2,596 prose sentences.**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | **16.6 words** |
| Median | **14 words** |
| Standard deviation | **10.9** (high — deliberate variance) |
| Range | 1 to 110 words |

**Length distribution:**

| Band | Share |
|---|---|
| 1–5 words | 10.2% |
| 6–10 words | 23.0% |
| 11–15 words | 21.1% |
| 16–20 words | 16.8% |
| 21–30 words | 18.8% |
| 31–45 words | 8.0% |
| 46+ words | 2.2% |

The shape that matters: **10.2% of sentences are five words or shorter, and 10.2% are thirty-one words or longer.** That symmetry is the engine. He does not write medium prose; he writes long observational sentences and then hits you with a four-word one. 44% of all sentences fall in the 6–15 word band — that is the cruising speed, and the extremes are the gearchanges.

**Fragments.** Rarer than the rhythm suggests. True verbless fragments are perhaps 1–2% of sentences. What he *does* use, heavily, is the **very short complete sentence** — 140 sentences of ≤4 words (5.4%), 77 of ≤3 words. `You descend.` `You walk on.` `Nothing happens.` `You flee.` `Blood is everywhere.` `You are alive.` `Then you blacken.` `Do something!` The effect reads as fragmentary but is grammatically whole. This matters for imitation: **do not write fragments; write tiny sentences with a subject and a verb.** The two real fragment tricks he does use are the isolated one-word paragraph (`Nothing.`) and the trailing repetition (`Down, down, down...`, `And kick...`).

**Semicolons: 20 in 44,550 words** — roughly one every 2,200 words. Effectively absent. Do not use them.

**Dashes: 113** (110 spaced en-dashes ` – `, 3 double-hyphens). Dashes outnumber semicolons **5.6 to 1**. This is his one piece of heavy punctuation, and he uses it for three things:
- self-correction mid-perception: `A sound ... comes from behind you – or at least, you think it comes from behind you.`
- the withheld noun: `You hear – voices.` / `a pile of – of people.`
- the swerve into bathos or revelation: `they're more than happy to chew their way through your canoe to get at it – to get at you.`

**Colons: 56. Parentheses: 52 opens.** The parentheses are the narrator's asides — `(and then several more, in case the room is about to flood)`. Charming in moderation; see §6.

**Commas: 3,190** — 1.23 per sentence. He is a comma writer, not a semicolon writer, and roughly 179 of those commas are doing work a semicolon or a period would normally do. **Comma splices are habitual and mostly deliberate**, almost always in the short appositional form where a perception is tagged with the act of perceiving:
> `April 1934, it says.` · `It's leather, you think.` · `At least, you think it's a gate.` · `NAUE TOURN, they say.`

This is a signature. Keep it. The failure mode is the *other* kind of comma-join — the 45-word sentence that hangs four subordinate clauses off one another and loses its own subject.

**Front-load or back-load?** **Back-load, decisively.** The punch is at the end of the sentence, of the paragraph, and of the node. He builds the observation and then detonates the last four words:
> `Being watched by anyone is always a little unnerving, but the idea of being watched by spiders is horrific. They have so many eyes.`

Only 33 sentences open with a participial phrase. Only 34 open with `Then`. **25.7% of sentences open with "You"**, and 505 open with `The`/`A`/`An`. The default sentence is *subject-verb-object, then the twist at the end.*

**Exclamation marks in narration: 42** — nearly all inside quoted dialogue. The narrator almost never shouts. (The *choice buttons* are full of exclamation marks — that is the player's voice, and it is a different register entirely. See §4.)

## 2. DICTION

**Register: middle-plain American, with two controlled departures.** The baseline is unfussy contemporary prose — a person telling you what happened. He departs upward into Latinate/archaic diction only when a supernatural authority speaks (`thee/thou/thy` appears 16 times, `whence/thence/hither/fain/ere/yore` 7, and essentially all of it is in the Dante and Hell sections, in dialogue). He departs downward into flippancy at deaths and choice-points. The narrating voice in between is level.

**Profanity: zero.** No `fuck`, no `shit`, in 44,550 words. `damn` 12, `hell` 26 (mostly literal). This is a clean-mouthed narrator. Do not add profanity to imitate him; it is not there.

**The load-bearing vocabulary** (raw counts in prose):

*Architecture and enclosure* — `room` 101, `door` 85, `floor` 86, `wall` 84 / `walls` 78, `ceiling` 69, `stone` 80, `passage` 31, `hall` 30, `stairs` 45, `hole` 30, `archway`/`arch`, `keystone`, `portcullis`, `sconce`, `masonry`, `alcove`.

*Water* — `water` 111, `river` 32, `surface` 33, `current(s)/rapids/undertow/undercurrent/whitewater` 37 combined, `waterfall`, `falls`, `whirlpool` 4, `vortex` 3, `drown*` 6, `brine` 2, `eddies`.

*Nautical/maritime specifics* (these are precise and correctly used) — `canoe` 57, `paddle` 39, `quay` 6, `gaff` 6, `bulwark` 4, `piling(s)` 3, `stern` 4, `prow` 1, `bowline` 1, `cleat` 1, `berthed` 1, `moored` 2, `tackles` 2, `anchor` 1, `flotsam` 1, `scallops` 1, `barnacles` 1. He knows the words and does not show off with them — one per paragraph, never a list.

*Light and dark* — `light` 74, `torch*` 43, `darkness` 35, `dark` 30, `moss` 16 (always glowing, always green/emerald/lime), `glowing`, `flicker*`, `dim`.

*Body* — anatomical nouns 70 (`bone`, `skull`, `spine`, `flesh`, `marrow`, `mandible`, `abdomen`, `thorax`, `capillaries`, `tendon`, `vestigial`), `blood`/`gore`/`scarlet` 45, `eyes` 61, `head` 63, `face` 59, `hands` 34, `feet` 45. `boots` 31 — the player's boots are a running motif, mentioned in almost every locomotion beat.

*Scale words* — `immense/enormous/gigantic/titanic/mammoth/colossal/gargantuan` 27 combined. Used sparingly enough to still land.

**Sense hierarchy — the single most important diction fact:**

| Sense | Tokens | Per 10k |
|---|---|---|

**He hears before he sees.** Sound leads sight by 16%, and structurally it leads it in time too: the noise arrives, then the source. His sound vocabulary is his best vocabulary and it is almost entirely *by-analogy to industry and domestic objects*: `a wrench being thrown at a steel plate`, `a sledgehammer against an anvil`, `sandpaper against sandpaper`, `hundreds of people using chalkboards`, `the crunching of walnuts in a nutcracker`, `water from a fire hose against a drum`, `a slow rain`, a `slurping`, a `tink, tink`, a `clop, clop`, a `rasp, rasp, rasp`.

**Doubling as an intensifier** is a genuine tic and a good one used at 1–2 per thousand words: `a long, long time`, `down, down, down`, `very, very slick`, `an old, old ladder`, `many, many, many generations`.

## 3. HOW HE HANDLES HORROR

**The core technique: sound first, source second, and often no source at all.** He introduces a threat as an unattributed noise, lets it recur across several nodes without explanation, and only names it when the player walks into it. The scratching/sandpaper sound recurs across ~six nodes before you meet the spiders. This is the best thing in the book and the most transferable.

**Does he name the thing?** Yes — eventually, and usually too much. The approach is superb and the revelation is where he over-writes. The great spider gets a 45-word clause-pile at L941 that is genuinely purple; the *approach* to it (`Instantly, the light goes out. A moment later and it returns...`) is flawless. **Imitate the approach, cut the reveal to a third.**

**Ratio of implication to statement:** roughly 3:1 during approach, inverting to 1:4 at the moment of death. The deaths are explicit, anatomical, and often gleeful. That inversion is deliberate — dread is withheld, consequence is not — but see §6 on the jokes.

**Bodies:** yes, in anatomical specifics rather than gore-adjectives. Fingertips flayed and glued by dried blood. A jaw resting on `a rigid, frozen falls of gore`. Eyes bursting and rolling down cheeks. He prefers *one exact wrong detail* to a wash of viscera — when he remembers to.

**Smell** is his ace and he under-uses it (only 40 tokens). Every time he reaches for it, it works: burning maple syrup, ammonia, cedarwood, `an old but seldom-used locker room`, and the best one — the layered gore-smell in the surgery. **Use smell more than he did.**

**No sound is a sound.** `You kick a stone over the edge. The sound of nothing practically deafens you.`

### Exemplary passages, labelled by technique

**Understated dread — flat closing clause carries the horror**
> `the idea of being watched by spiders is horrific. They have so many eyes.` *(L231)*

**Sound before source, plus self-doubt as amplifier**
> `A sound like sandpaper against sandpaper comes from behind you – or at least, you think it comes from behind you.` *(L605)*

**Specificity as revelation — the general dread resolves into one exact object**
> `It is, without a doubt, the pads of two human fingers, flayed from their flesh and glued together by dried blood` *(L1061)*

**Correcting the expected sense-impression into a worse one**
> `not the smell of death and decay, but the odor and stink of one drying coat of gore over another` *(L1065)*

**Atrocity buried in a subordinate clause, delivered with a flat verb and no reaction**
> `“Jes' his mos' precious thing in de whole, wide world,” she says, and then flings the child bodily out the window.` *(L2691)*

**Horror as neutral observation; the player's non-response is the beat**
> `a young girl with her hair in pigtails jumping up and down on a pile of broken glass` *(L2977)* — followed three lines later by `You don't wave back.`

**Time made visible on a living body**
> `Cobwebs have formed connecting the bodice of her dress and her chin, and spill off the tea saucer in her hand` *(L2933)*

**The threat that was already there, below you, the whole time**
> `just beneath the waterline, are a crowd of wide, open mouths full of sharp, triangular teeth` *(L4336)*

**Sound arrives before impact — the ear is the last accurate sense**
> `You hear a wave slap stone with a loud smack just a moment before you are smashed into a sharp edge` *(L1699)*

**Domestic simile against atrocity; indifference is the horror, not appetite**
> `It merely sucks on Mikhail's leg as if it were a chicken bone and stares out of its round, white, vestigial eyes` *(L4203)*

**Anatomical geometry instead of adjectives**
> `Their open mouths make wide equators around their faces and display many rows of small, sharp, triangular teeth.` *(L3021)*

**Two-beat parallel close; one word swapped, nothing explained**
> `Then you are somewhere else. Then you are someone else.` *(L1265)*

## 4. SECOND PERSON

`you`/`your`/`you're`/`yourself` account for **3,017 tokens — 6.8% of every word in the book.** 25.7% of all sentences begin with `You`. Of 217 story nodes, 78 open on `You`, 41 on `The`, 20 on `This`. So roughly **36% of nodes open on the player's body or action; 28% open on the room.**

**He tells you what you perceive, not what you feel.** This is the discipline that makes it work and it is measurable:

| Construction | Count |
|---|---|
| `you feel` | 55 |
| `you see` | 48 |
| `you hear` | 43 |
| `you think` | 31 |
| `you know` | 20 |
| `you realize` | 13 |
| `you smell` | 6 |
| **`you are/you're` + emotion adjective** (terrified, horrified, relieved…) | **7** |

Seven. In fifty thousand words. He almost never says *you are afraid* — he says what makes you afraid and stops. The `you feel` count is misleading: most of those are physical (`you feel the ground`, `you feel yourself pulled forward`), not emotional.

**The hedged-perception construction** is how he keeps the player's interiority honest: `you get the impression` (11), `it would seem` (7), `you find yourself` (19), `you're not sure, but` — these attribute the interpretation to the player rather than asserting it. Overused (see §6), but structurally correct: *the narrator is inside the player's head and does not know more than the player does.*

**Agency in the prose.** `you cannot` / `you can't` appears **49 times**. He is comfortable removing agency in narration — you slide, you are swept, you cannot stop yourself — and then handing it back sharply at the decision point with an unadorned imperative:
> `You need to do something, right now.` · `Do something!` · `You have to decide right now.` · `Get ready to move.` · `You must choose a door.`

Direct rhetorical questions to the player: **83 question marks.** `What do you think? Plywood and spiders, or lemonade?` · `Which exit will you take?` · `What will you do?`

**The register split.** The narration is calm and third-person-ish in its restraint. The *choice text* is written in an excitable first person with exclamation marks — `[This old sewer's awesome! I want to check out those metal covers.]` This is a deliberate two-voice design: **the narrator does not get excited; the player does.** For Fathom, that means UI/choice strings can carry personality the prose refuses to.

**Occasional second-person past-life intrusion.** In the Insight passages he switches the "you" from the player-character to a *different* person's memory and lets you inhabit it without warning (`Suddenly you are standing with the stone angled carefully at the wall in your left hand`, L4191). Extremely effective. Cheap to do. Steal it.

## 5. RHYTHM AND PARAGRAPHING

**Paragraph length: mean 44 words, median 39.** Distribution:

| Length | Share |
|---|---|
| ≤15 words | 24.7% |
| 16–40 words | 27.0% |
| 41–80 words | 34.2% |
| 81–140 words | 13.1% |
| 141+ words | 1.1% |

**Sentences per paragraph: mean 2.7, median 2.** **28.6% of paragraphs are a single sentence. 23.7% are two.** Only 7.1% run to six sentences or more.

So the page shape is: a substantial 3–5 sentence descriptive block, then a one-sentence paragraph landing on it. That one-sentence paragraph is doing the same job as the short sentence inside a paragraph, one level up.

**How he opens a node.** Three patterns, in rough order of frequency:
1. **Player-first:** `You duck into the alcove and brace yourself against the walls.`
2. **Room-first, one clause:** `The chamber is a round, featureless one with a flat ceiling and floor.`
3. **Sensation-first:** `The air is musty and smells vaguely sweet, like maple syrup burning in a frying pan.` / `You are first aware of salt.`

He almost never opens with weather, backstory, or an abstraction. The first sentence puts a body in a space.

**How he closes a node.** Almost always: a short paragraph that either (a) states the danger flatly, (b) asks the player a direct question, or (c) undercuts with a wry aside. Examples of the flat-danger close: `This place is a deathtrap.` · `You're being chased, though. Of that you are sure.` · `The danger is very real, very present, and very, very serious.`

**How he lands an ending beat.** All 39 deaths end on a short closing sentence set as its own paragraph, after the description of the death has finished. The good ones are cold:
> `Then you are no more.` · `In this one, you are dead.` · `Then you burn. Then you blacken.` · `Your foot decays within it.` · `Nothing.`

The weak ones are jokes (`Maybe canoeing isn't your thing.` · `This river has a heart of stone.` · `After all, you could have looked it up.`). Both patterns are unmistakably his; only one is worth keeping (see §6).

**The descent rhythm.** When he wants motion over distance he abandons sentence variety entirely and repeats: `Down, down you go, slowly and for a long, long time.` · `Down, down, down...` · `Step by step, lower and lower.` · `this one – no, but maybe this one – well, this one, perhaps`. This is his best tool for making a long traverse feel long without making it boring. **For a submarine game this transfers directly to depth and to sonar returns.**

## 6. TICS AND WEAKNESSES

Be honest about these; the goal is to imitate his best, not his average.

**1. Compulsive hedging.** `seem*` 118, `perhaps` 52, `almost` 40, `you get the impression` 11, `it would seem` 7, `not unlike` 5. That is ~230 hedges in 44.5k words — one every 190 words. It starts as an epistemic device (the player genuinely doesn't know) and degrades into a verbal reflex that softens description that should be hard. **A wall that "seems to be made of stone" is a wall the writer didn't commit to.** Budget: allow yourself one hedge per 500 words and make it count.

**2. Narrator interjections.** `however` 53, `naturally` 12, `of course` 11, `anyhow/anyway` 10, `indeed` 9, `in any case` 8 — plus 52 parentheticals. A fussy, buttonholing narrator who keeps stepping in front of the scene to editorialise. In the first act it's charming. By the Hell section it's a mannerism.

**3. Simile inflation and register-collapse.** ~156 explicit comparisons (`like a/the` 104 + `as if` 52), roughly one every 285 words. The best are extraordinary: `Barnacles climb them like small, black mountains.` · `their tortured cries shrill and undistinguished` · `like the feelers of a bizarre anemone`. But a third of them deflate the register at the worst possible moment — `like a goldfish flushed down a toilet`, `like an Olympic gymnast on the parallel bars`, `like the Eiffel Tower`, `like a human pinball`, `murder by garbage disposal, and breakdancing on broken glass`, `You feel like Indiana Jones, or Sherlock Holmes or something`. **Rule: if a simile makes the reader picture a sitcom, it has cost you the scene.**

**4. The death joke.** 39 endings; roughly a third close on a gag. `Maybe canoeing isn't your thing.` · `In your next life, you become a lifeguard. In this one, you are dead.` · `who brings boxing to an archery fight?` · `Cause of Death: Sleeping in a Tremendously Treacherous Environment.` This is a real voice and it is genuinely funny, but at that density death stops costing anything, and a horror game that makes dying free has no horror. **Ration: one wry death per five. The rest end cold.**

**5. Purple at the reveal.** The moment a monster is fully visible, the clauses stack. The great spider (L941) runs four appositive clauses over ~90 words. `Your parts are ingested, digested, and emerge again variously as portions of portions of portions...` (L961). The approach is always better than the arrival.

**6. Inventory dumps.** The manor rooms are furniture catalogues — L2373 is a 213-word paragraph listing a bed, screen, desk, mirror, bottles, tins, brush, hat rack, dresser, wardrobe, slippers, divan, book, tea. Only two of those objects matter. When he selects (`the teaspoon on the saucer has a brown drop of tea in it`) it is devastating; when he lists, it is furniture.

**7. Telling you the room's mood.** `Everything about this chamber feels wrong to you` · `you feel invasive` · `the effect is like that of a giant scarlet candle`. Always weaker than the object he has just described. Cut the summary sentence; the detail already did it.

**8. Copy-paste.** 24 paragraphs of >60 characters appear more than once, 38 duplicate copies in all — the canoe-boarding paragraph, the fish-mouths paragraph, the quay descriptions, and the `A4`–`A22` maze (nineteen nodes of identical text). Structural laziness, and it inflates the frequency of the sentences involved. Do not learn rhythm from those.

**9. Genuine errors worth not copying.** `whom` used as a subject 5+ times (`Few are they whom venture into hell`, `it was you whom saved the people`, `your wife whom always loved you`). Stalagmites described as *hanging from the ceiling* — twice (L1057, L4039); they rise from the floor. `it's bulwarks` for `its` (L1641). `the affect is the same` (L2087). `You read headlong toward the centaur` (L5820). `Fhtagn Foundary` (L367) — a nice joke, an accidental misspelling of *foundry*.

**10. Structural, not stylistic, but fatal:** the book changes genre three times (urban-underground → Southern-Gothic ghost story → Dante's *Inferno*) and each transition is a hard cut. Whatever else Fathom takes, take one world and stay in it.

## 7. TEN RULES

1. **Lead with sound.** Introduce every threat as an unattributed noise, compared to a piece of machinery or a kitchen object, and name its source at least one paragraph later — or never.
2. **Write short complete sentences, not fragments.** Aim for one sentence of four words or fewer every ten sentences; give it a subject and a verb; put it at the end of the paragraph.
3. **Put the punch in the last four words of the sentence, the last sentence of the paragraph, and the last paragraph of the passage.** Never front-load a revelation.
4. **Report perception, never emotion.** Say what the player sees, hears, smells and touches. Do not write *you are afraid* — you get seven of those in the whole game, spend them like money.
5. **Ban the semicolon; ration the dash.** One dash per ~400 words, used only to self-correct a perception, withhold a noun, or swerve at the last instant.
6. **One hedge per 500 words.** Delete *seems*, *perhaps*, *almost*, *appears to be*, *you get the impression* on sight unless the player's uncertainty is the point of the sentence.
7. **One simile per 300 words, and it must not be funny.** Compare to industry, weather, anatomy, or the sea. If it evokes a TV show, a sport, or a landmark, cut it.
8. **Choose one exact wrong object per room and describe only that.** A steaming cup under a quarter-inch of dust. A boot with a foot still decaying in it. Do not inventory the furniture.
9. **End death cold four times out of five.** `Then you are no more.` `In this one, you are dead.` `Your foot decays within it.` Save the wry ending for the fifth.
10. **Use repetition for distance.** When the player travels far, repeat a word three times — *down, down, down* / *deeper, and deeper, and deeper* — rather than describing three new things.

## 8. HARVESTABLE MATERIAL

Ordered roughly by usefulness to an undersea game. **⚓ = directly reusable underwater/drowning/nautical.**

1. **⚓ The submerged mouths under the waterline** — L4336, L4356. Gray fish-beings standing on the lake bed beneath a waterfall, heads thrown back, mouths open, waiting with `infinite patience` for whatever comes over the falls. Blind, white, vestigial eyes. The single best image in the book and it is already aquatic.
2. **⚓ Reading ripples to locate what's below you** — L4368: `you can tell what side of your canoe the fish-men are on by subtle ripples on the surface`. A whole sonar/passive-detection mechanic, already written as prose.
3. **⚓ The cobwebbed quay** — L1605–1609. `Thick ropes lie in ancient, cobwebbed coils here and there along the dock, some of them trailing into the black, shimmering water.` A rusted anchor, a gaff with a rotten handle, no boats. Perfect derelict-dock/moonpool description.
4. **⚓ Barnacles and scallops on the pilings** — L1633, L1671. `Scallops cling to the pilings in thick clumps near the waterline.` / `Barnacles climb them like small, black mountains.`
5. **⚓ Mikhail and the fish-man** — L4195–4207. A dead comrade's leg being gnawed; the survivor hammering a warning into the rock wall with two stones while it happens; the creature `not the least bit concerned you might escape`. The warning-carved-by-a-previous-diver is a complete environmental-storytelling pattern.
